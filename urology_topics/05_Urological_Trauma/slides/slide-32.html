UROLOGICAL TRAUMA

Urological Trauma — Key Takeaways

Genitourinary injuries: ~10% of all emergency-room injuries; early diagnosis prevents serious complications; resuscitation and hemorrhage control come first; examine the urethral meatus for blood before any catheter.
Renal trauma: the most common urinary injury — blunt in 80–85%; grades 1–5 (contusion → pedicle avulsion); hematuria does not reflect severity; CT is the staging gold standard; non-visualization = thrombosis/avulsion; 85% of blunt injuries are managed conservatively; surgery for persistent bleeding, extravasation, nonviable tissue, pedicle injury (< 5%); penetrating wounds are explored.
Ureteral injury: rare — pelvic surgery, endoscopic baskets, gunshots, deceleration avulsion; partial division → early extravasation/ileus/urinoma, late fistula/stricture/hydronephrosis; bilateral ligation → anuria; 90% microhematuria with external violence; US best for early postoperative detection; repair — debridement, spatulated tension-free anastomosis, stent, drain; delayed repair at 7–10 days.
Bladder injury: pelvic fracture in 90% of ruptures; extraperitoneal (fracture fragments) vs intraperitoneal (blow on full bladder); ascending cystogram — fill 300 mL, drain, repeat film; extraperitoneal → catheter 10 days; intraperitoneal → transperitoneal repair; avoid entering the pelvic hematoma.
Posterior urethra (membranous, prostate-membranous junction): blood at the meatus is the cardinal sign; never catheterize; ascending urethrogram 20–30 mL; types I–IV; suprapubic cystostomy 3 months → delayed single-stage reconstruction (stricture < 5%, impotence 30–35%, incontinence < 2%); immediate realignment carries higher complication rates.
Anterior urethra (bulbous, straddle): extravasation limited by Colles' fascia (scrotum → penis → abdominal wall); urethrogram 15–20 mL; no voiding, no catheter, suprapubic cystostomy; minor extravasation → voiding study at 7 days; extensive → 2–3 weeks; drainage of extravasated areas + antibiotics; stricture usually needs no surgery unless flow is significantly reduced.

Trauma management follows one rule — image before you instrument, and stage before you operate.
