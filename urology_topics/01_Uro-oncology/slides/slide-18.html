Bladder Cancer · 6/9

Diagnostic Workup

Cytology, imaging and endoscopic evaluation

Conventional Microscopic Cytology

  • Malignant urothelial cells observed in urinary sediment or bladder washings
  • Tumour cells have large nuclei with irregular, coarsely textured chromatin
  • Limitations: cells from well-differentiated tumours look cytologically normal and are more cohesive (not readily shed)
  • More sensitive in high-grade tumours or carcinoma in situ
  • May be falsely negative in 20% even with high-grade tumours

Excretory Urography (IVU)

  • Indicated in all patients with signs and symptoms suggestive of bladder cancer
  • Not a sensitive means of detecting bladder tumours, particularly small ones
  • Useful for examining the upper urinary tracts for associated urothelial tumours
  • Large tumours may appear as filling defects in the bladder on the cystogram phase
  • Ureteral obstruction by a bladder tumour usually signals muscle-invasive cancer

Cystoscopy & Biopsy

  • All patients suspected of having bladder cancer should have careful cystoscopy and bimanual examination
  • Abnormal areas should be biopsied; random or selected-site mucosal biopsy specimens may also be obtained
  • Retrograde pyelography if the upper tracts are not adequately visualized on the excretory urogram
  • Ideal resection: resect the bulk of the tumour, then the deep portion with underlying bladder muscle, sending each specimen separately for histology

Cross-Sectional Imaging

  • CT scan — assesses extent of primary tumour, pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenopathy and visceral metastases; should be done before TUR to accurately assess depth of penetration
  • MRI scan — not much more helpful than CT scanning
  • Ultrasonography — visualizes intravesical growth; used for follow-up of upper urinary tract dilatation due to obstruction by the tumour
