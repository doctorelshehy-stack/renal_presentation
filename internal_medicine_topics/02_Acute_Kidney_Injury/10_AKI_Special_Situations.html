Contrast-Induced Nephropathy (CIN)

Part 1 — Contrast-Induced Nephropathy

CIN — Definition

Definition

CIN is acute kidney injury characterized by rise of serum creatinine of ≥ 0.5 mg/dl or 25% above the base line within 48 hours after contrast administration.

Part 1 — Contrast-Induced Nephropathy

CIN — Risk Factors

Patient-related factors

  • Pre-existing CKD — the most potent risk factor. Nearly 60% of patients developing CIN have CKD, and the incidence of CIN parallels the severity of preexisting renal impairment.
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Congestive heart failure
  • Hypotension
  • Volume depletion
  • Old age

Contrast-related factors

  • Large volume (dose) of parenteral contrast material.
  • The type of contrast material, especially its osmolarity — the incidence of CIN is high with the high osmolar contrast materials.
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 1 — Contrast-Induced Nephropathy

CIN — Pathogenesis

Several mechanisms have been proposed:

Renal ischemia

Contrast media induce renal ischemia by two mechanisms:

  • Production of VC compounds as endothelin and adenosine.
  • Increased oxygen utilization in the renal tubules.

Hyperosmolarity

Intra-tubular hyperosmolarity causes:

  • Increase in intra-tubular hydrostatic pressure, which decreases glomerular filtration.
  • Increase in tubular cell apoptosis.

Oxygen free radicals

Generation of oxygen free radicals.

Direct cellular toxicity

Direct toxic effect on tubular cells.

Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 1 — Contrast-Induced Nephropathy

CIN — Diagnosis

Symptoms and signs

  • In the vast majority of patients, there are no symptoms or signs.
  • In a smaller subset of patients, there is oliguria with or without volume overload.
  • Rarely, a patient with CIN may present with symptoms and signs of uremia.

Laboratory — serum creatinine

  • In the majority of patients, the serum creatinine level begins to rise 24–48 hours after the contrast media exposure, peaks within 3–5 days and returns to the base line level within 7–10 days.
  • In more severe cases, the serum creatinine does not peak until 5–10 days and may be accompanied by oliguria and requirement for dialysis.

Imaging

To exclude other causes of AKI.

Part 1 — Contrast-Induced Nephropathy

CIN — Prevention (1/2)

  1. Use non-iodinated contrast media.
  2. Use low osmolar contrast media.
  3. Minimize contrast volume (dose). A formula based on body weight and baseline S. creatinine is used to limit the volume of contrast media: Volume of contrast = 5 ml of contrast/kg of BW (maximum 300 ml) / S. creatinine mg/dl.
  4. Space between contrast administrations.
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 1 — Contrast-Induced Nephropathy

CIN — Prevention (2/2)

  1. Avoid nephrotoxic drugs before and after the procedure.
  2. Adequate hydration before, during, and after the procedure.
  3. Use of N-acetyl cysteine — it is unclear whether N-acetyl cysteine prevents CIN as studies are conflicting.
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 1 — Contrast-Induced Nephropathy

CIN — Treatment

There is no specific therapy for CIN once it occurs. The best strategy is one of prevention. However, once a patient develops CIN the following should be taken:

  1. Manage fluid and electrolytes.
  2. Adjust medications that are eliminated by the kidney.
  3. Regular monitoring of electrolytes, S. creatinine and BUN.
  4. Dialysis as needed.

Part 1 — Contrast-Induced Nephropathy

CIN — Prognosis

  • In most patients, complete recovery of renal function usually occurs.
  • A small minority of patients may go on to CKD.
✓
Key message

Because treatment options are limited, the best strategy for CIN is one of prevention.

Part 2 — Tumor Lysis Syndrome

Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)

Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 2 — Tumor Lysis Syndrome

TLS — Definition

Definition

It is an acute critical illness characterized by severe hyperuricemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperkalemia, hypocalcemia, and AKI seen in patients with rapidly growing cancers (especially Burkett's lymphomas) after administration of chemotherapeutic agents.

The acute electrolyte disorders are due to massive sudden death of tumor cells → release of intra-cellular electrolytes (uric acid, phosphorus, and potassium). Hypocalcemia is secondary to hyperphosphatemia to maintain calcium-phosphorus balance.

Part 2 — Tumor Lysis Syndrome

TLS — Patients at Risk & Clinical Features

Patients at risk

  1. Hematological and lymphoproliferative malignancy with marked elevation of serum LDH.
  2. Volume depletion.
  3. Acidic urinary pH.
  4. Patients with CKD — because renal clearance is the primary mechanism of excretion of UA and phosphates.

Clinical features

Varying degrees of AKI due to intra-tubular precipitation of UA (acute UA nephropathy) and/or acute nephrocalcinosis secondary to marked hyper-phosphatemia.

AKI is most marked during the induction of chemotherapy.

Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 2 — Tumor Lysis Syndrome

TLS — Diagnosis & Differential Diagnosis

Diagnosis

  • Any patient with a known or suspected malignancy, especially Burkett's lymphoma, who presents with AKI, hyperuricemia, and elevated serum LDH should be examined for TLS.
  • The presence of concomitant volume depletion, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia and hypocalcemia strongly support the clinical diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis — other causes of AKI in cancer patients

  1. UT obstruction.
  2. Severe volume depletion.
  3. Parenchymal renal diseases: GN secondary to cryoglobimemia or tumor-related Ag-Ab complexes; vasculitis; hypercalcemic nephropathy; tumor infiltrating the kidney parenchyma; myeloma kidney (cast nephropathy).
  4. Nephrotoxic drugs: Methotrexate, cisplatinum, mitomycin C, INF-Alfa, antibiotics.
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 2 — Tumor Lysis Syndrome

TLS — Prevention (Prophylactic)

Early identification of patients at risk, and institution of the following measures prior to anti-neoplastic drug therapy:

  1. Correct initial electrolyte and fluid disorders.
  2. Maintain adequate hydration and urine output.
  3. Alkaline urine to pH > 7.0 to enhance UA solubility and prevent precipitation.
  4. Give oral phosphate binding antacids.
  5. Allopurinol (300 mg/m²) 1–2 days before chemotherapy.
  6. Correct any renal or pre-renal dysfunction, if present.
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 2 — Tumor Lysis Syndrome

TLS — Treatment of Established AKI (1/2)

  1. Administer allopurinol, 600 mg/day.
  2. Intravenous isotonic NaHco3 at rate of 200–300 ml/h to expand volume, wash out the renal medulla, and alkalinize the urine.
  3. Dialysis: HD is life saving in the management of TLS and should be considered for every patient. It is generally required during induction.
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 2 — Tumor Lysis Syndrome

TLS — Treatment of Established AKI (2/2)

Main goals of HD

  • Decrease plasma levels of UA, phosphorus, and potassium.
  • Restore volume overload.
  • Control of uremia.

Post-chemotherapy measures

  • Discontinue urine alkalization when UA homeostasis is achieved to avoid Ca3Po4 precipitation.
  • Treat symptomatic hypocalcaemia after correction of hyperphosphatemia.

Part 3 — Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis — Definition

Definition

Rhabdomyolysis is a disorder resulting from an injury or a metabolic defect in the skeletal muscle cell resulting in lysis of the cell membrane and leakage of its contents (myoglobin, enzymes, phosphorus, potassium) into the blood.

Part 3 — Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis — Causes (1/2)

Direct trauma

  • Crush syndrome
  • Electrical shock
  • Prolonged pressure with coma
  • Thermal burns & freezing

Excessive exercise

e.g. athletic injury and convulsive seizures.

Hereditary myopathies

e.g. myophosphorylase deficiency (McArdles diseases).

Acquired metabolic disorders

  • Hyperthyroidism
  • Hypokalemia (acute)
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis
  • Hypophosphatemia (acute)
  • Alcoholism
  • Hyponatremia (acute)
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 3 — Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis — Causes (2/2)

Hypoxia and ischemia

  • Carbon monoxide poisoning
  • Vascular occlusion
  • Atheromatous embolism
  • Compartment syndrome

Drugs

  • Statins and lipid-lowering drugs
  • Cocaine
  • Amphetamine derivatives

Infectious diseases

Viral: Coxsacki, HIV, Influenza. Bacterial: Clostridia infection, Legionella, Streptococcal infection, Staphylococcal infection, Pneumococcal pneumonia.

Toxins

Snake venom, poisonous mushrooms, fish poisoning (Haff disease).

Miscellaneous

Malignant hyperthermia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

Rhabdomyolysis — Pathogenesis (Mild / Moderate)

Mild and moderate rhabdomyolysis

e.g. after intense exertion, violent repetitive activities or a grand mal seizure → direct injury of muscle cells and depletion of energy stores → muscle pain and weakness.

⚠
Severe acute rhabdomyolysis

In severe acute cases (crush injury / pigmented nephropathy), AKI develops — mechanisms on the next slide.

Part 3 — Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis — Pathogenesis of AKI

In severe acute rhabdomyolysis (crush injury / pigmented nephropathy), AKI is due to:

1. Renal ischemia

Myoglobin has an intense VC (vasoconstrictor) effect → renal ischemia.

2. Tubular obstruction and injury

Once the myoglobin molecule is filtered by the glomerulus and enters the PCT fluid:

  • a. A portion enters into the PC cells. Inside the cells the molecule releases elemental iron and iron compounds forming toxic products that injure the cells.
  • b. The remaining unabsorbed pigment passes to distal nephron and interacts with Tamm-Hors Fall protein (in acidic urine) forming a gel and obstructs urine flow. Then, the concentration of pigment in the tubule rises, augmenting proximal tubular absorption and toxicity.
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 3 — Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis — Diagnosis

Clinical features

Depend on the severity of muscle injury:

  • Mild and moderate cases: muscular pain, tenderness, edema, stiffness, weakness and impaired mobility.
  • Severe acute cases (crush injury): AKI.

Laboratory — serum Creatine Kinase (CK)

  • Elevated total serum CK levels > 500 IU/L → high suspicion of acute rhabdomyolysis.
  • The CK-MM isoform is the most sensitive test to confirm the diagnosis.
Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Part 3 — Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis — Treatment

Severe cases should be managed in ICU.

  1. Aggressive and urgent volume replacement to maintain organ perfusion — but caution must be taken to avoid volume overload and pulmonary edema.
  2. Alkalinization of urine with bicarb infusion to prevent obstructive cast formation.
  3. Special care to respiratory failure due to diaphragmatic weakness if severe.
  4. Monitor for hyperkalemia by ECG and serum potassium level.
  5. Dialysis for severe AKI.

Part 3 — Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis — Prognosis

Prognosis

Patients who survive acute rhabdomyolysis are at risk of permanent disability (muscle fibrosis).
