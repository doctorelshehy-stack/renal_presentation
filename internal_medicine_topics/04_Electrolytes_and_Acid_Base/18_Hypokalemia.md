# Hypokalemia

**Source:** Internal Medicine Nephrology (Principles of Nephrology) – Dr. Hassan Abd-Elhady, Menoufia University

---

#### Definition: A decrease in the S. potassium concentration to a level below 3.5 mmol/L

---

#### Causes:

1- **Spurious hypokalemia;** Occurs when blood sample is kept in blood tube for long period at room temperature. This allows K+ to enter the cells → falsely lower serum K+ level. This is particularly seen with marked leukocytosis and when insulin is given just prior to blood drawing.

2- **Decreased K+ intake** (normal daily K+ intake = 40 – 120 mmol).

3- **Shift of K+ into the cells;** e.g. Alkalosis, insulin therapy, β agonists and stress.

4- **Renal K+ loss;** e.g.
   - Diuretics → activates renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS) hyperaldosteronism → salt retention and potassium loss.
   - Hyperaldosternism/ steroids therapy.
   - Proximal RTA
   - Hypomagnesaemia.
   - Polyuria.

5- **Extra-renal K+ loss;** e.g.
   - Loss of gastric fluid (persistent vomiting and gastric aspiration) leads to:
     - Loss of H+ → increases plasma bicarbonate → alkalosis → ↑ cellular K+ uptake.
     - Coexisting volume depletion → increases aldosterone secretion.

---

#### NB: Syndromes associated with hypokalemia are:

- **Hypokalemic periodic paralysis;**
  - Results form a defect in a voltage-gated calcium channel.
  - It is triggered by; strenuous exercise followed by rest, high carbohydrates or sodium meals, and sudden changes in temperature.

- **Bartter's syndrome;**
  - The defect is impaired Na Cl reabsorption in the loop of Henle.
  - Salt loss leading to volume depletion and activation of the RAAS. Findings are similar to administration of a loop diuretic.
  - Increased urinary calcium.
  - The syndrome is usually diagnosed in childhood, sometimes associated with growth and mental retardation.

- **Gitelman's syndrome:**
  - The defect is in the Na Cl transporter.
  - Findings mimic administration of a thiazide diuretic.

---

#### Manifestations:

**Symptoms:**
- GIT; nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, constipation.
- Muscles; muscle weakness or cramps, paralysis.
- CNS; paraesthesia, psychosis, delirium, hallucinations, depression.
- Renal; polyuria, nocturia → polydipsia.
- Cardiac; palpitation.

**Signs:**
- GIT; Ileus.
- CVS; hypotension, bradycardia or tachycardia, premature beats, arrhythmias.
- Respiratory; hypoventilation, respiratory distress, respiratory failure.
- CNS; mental changes (e.g. lethargy), decreased muscle strength, fasciculation, tetany, decreased tendon reflexes.

**ECG changes;** ST- segments and T- waves depression with prominent U- waves

ECG changes of hypokalemia.

---

#### Treatment:

- Control the cause.
- **Potassium therapy:**
  - **I.V. Potassium chloride infusion,** when serum K+ is < 2.5 mmol/L. The maximal rate of correction should be less than 20 mmol/hour.
  - **Oral potassium** is preferred route for K+ depletion in other cases.

**NB:**
Cellular shift of K+ into the cells requires less potassium to correct than hypokalemia from potassium loss.

- Check serum Mg levels, since it is difficult to restore potassium if the former is low.
- ECG monitoring is required.
