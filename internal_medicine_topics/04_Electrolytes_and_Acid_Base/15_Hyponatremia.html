Hyponatremia — Definition

Definition

Definition

A decrease in serum sodium concentration to a level below 135 mmol/L.

Dilutional hyponatremia

  • The most common form of the disorder.
  • Caused by water retention rather than sodium depletion.

Pseudohyponatremia

  • A spuriously low plasma sodium concentration — measured sodium is low, but the true physiological concentration is normal.
  • Occurs in any situation in which serum lipids or protein concentration is markedly increased (to maintain plasma osmolality).

Hyponatremia — Pathological Effects

Pathological Effects

  1. Hyponatremia leads to a shift of water from the ECF to the ICF compartments.
  2. Increased ICF volume leads to cell swelling.
  3. In the brain this leads to brain oedema and neurologic manifestations.
✎
Note

The severity of neurologic symptoms correlates well with the severity and the rate of development of hyponatremia.

Hyponatremia — Clinical Manifestations

Clinical Manifestations

Manifestations of the cause

Presenting features of the underlying disorder (e.g. heart failure, liver failure, SIADH).

Manifestations of hyponatremia (neurologic symptoms)

  • Changes in mental status: altered personality, lethargy, and confusion.
  • As the serum sodium falls: stupor, neuromuscular hyperexcitability, hyperreflexia, seizures, coma, and death.
⚠
Severity

The severity of neurologic symptoms correlates well with the severity and the rate of development of hyponatremia.

Hyponatremia — Classification

Classification by Plasma Osmolality (Tonicity)

Hyponatremia → three types according to plasma osmolality (tonicity)

  • Hypertonic — considered pseudohyponatremia.
  • Isotonic — considered pseudohyponatremia.
  • Hypotonic — true hyponatremia, classified into three subtypes according to the volume status:
    • Hypervolemic
    • Euvolemic
    • Hypovolemic

Part 4 — Diagnostic Algorithm

Diagnostic Algorithm

Internal Medicine — NephrologyPrinciples of Nephrology — Dr. H. Abd-Elhady

Hyponatremia — Diagnosis

Types and Causes of Hyponatremia — Flowchart

Hyponatremia → plasma osmolality

  • Low → extracellular volume status (EVS):
    • Increased → consider heart failure, cirrhosis or nephrosis.
    • Normal → urine osmolality:
      • < 100 mOsm/kg H2O → consider polydipsia.
      • > 100 mOsm/kg H2O → consider SIADH, hypothyroidism, or hypocortisolism.
    • Decreased → consider gastrointestinal fluid loss, Addison disease, salt-losing nephritis or cerebral salt wasting.
  • Normal or high → consider hyperglycemia or pseudohyponatremia.

Hyponatremia — Treatment

Treatment Principles

  1. Primary goal: control symptoms, then correct Na+ level.
  2. Determine whether hyponatremia is acute or chronic.
  3. Be careful of the rate of correction according to the rate of development (acute or chronic) to avoid osmotic demyelination, i.e. central pontine myelinolysis.
  4. The fluid of choice for correction depends on the volume status of the patient.

Key Takeaways

✓
Definition

Serum sodium < 135 mmol/L; the most common form is dilutional (water retention). Pseudohyponatremia is spurious — due to markedly increased serum lipids or protein.

✓
Classify first

By plasma osmolality (hypertonic and isotonic = pseudohyponatremia; hypotonic = true), then by volume status (hypervolemic, euvolemic, hypovolemic).

✓
Neurologic severity

Correlates with the severity and the rate of development — from altered mental status to stupor, seizures, coma and death.

✓
Correct slowly

Avoid rapid correction → osmotic demyelination (central pontine myelinolysis); fluid of choice depends on the volume status.

✓
Drugs

Isotonic 0.9% NaCl (hypo and euvolemic); hypertonic 3% NaCl + loop diuretics (hyper and euvolemic); vasopressin receptor antagonists (conivaptan, tolvaptan).
